What does a 12-step sponsor do? What AA, treatment centers and researchers say

By the AI Sponsor team • Published November 26, 2026 • 6 min read
The short answer

A sponsor is someone in your fellowship who has been in recovery for a while, usually a year or more, and who agrees to take you through the Twelve Steps. You talk one-on-one, often between meetings, and they share what worked for them. They aren't paid, and they aren't a counselor. They're another member who's a little further along.

If you're new to a 12-step program, people will probably tell you to get a sponsor before anyone explains what a sponsor actually does. We read what AA itself, a large US treatment provider, the Hazelden Betty Ford Foundation and recovery researchers have to say about it. They mostly agree, and each one adds something the others leave out.

What AA says

AA's pamphlet Questions and Answers on Sponsorship starts with how AA began. Bill W. had been sober for a few months when he badly wanted a drink, so he went looking for another alcoholic to talk to. That's how he met Dr. Bob. Nobody called it sponsorship back then, but that's what it was: one person with some time in recovery, sharing what they'd learned with someone newer.

According to the pamphlet, sponsor and newcomer meet as equals. A sponsor goes through the Steps with you, introduces you to other members, points you to the literature and is there for you when something comes up that could threaten your sobriety. AA sums up the main benefit simply: you have at least one person who fully understands what you're going through and cares about you.

The pamphlet is just as direct about the limits. A sponsor doesn't offer professional help, the kind you'd get from a counselor, doctor, lawyer or social worker, although they may help you find it. They shouldn't push their own beliefs on you, religious or otherwise. And a good sponsor doesn't pretend to have every answer.

AA has no fixed rules about who can sponsor, though it suggests a year or more without a drink. If the relationship isn't working, you can change sponsors. AA also says it's never too late to get one, even if you've been around the program for years.

What American Addiction Centers says

American Addiction Centers runs treatment centers around the US, and its blog describes a sponsor as a mentor and guide. That's usually someone with at least a year in recovery who helps you settle into the program, answers your questions, works the Steps with you and holds you accountable. Your sponsor is also someone you can talk to about things you'd rather not bring up in a meeting.

AAC is most helpful on the everyday side of being a sponsee. Early on, agree with your sponsor on when it's OK to call and whether they'd rather get a text or an email. Keep calls to a minimum, show up when you've arranged to meet, and do the step work you agreed on in between. AAC also reminds sponsees that a sponsor is a guide, not a therapist, and won't know everything.

What Hazelden Betty Ford says

The Hazelden Betty Ford Foundation describes AA as peer-based mutual help rather than addiction treatment. Members usually learn to work the Steps with a sponsor, who supports them one-on-one in addition to group meetings. Working the Steps never really ends, Hazelden adds. There's no final "completion" to reach.

Hazelden's article lists many of the fellowships that adapted AA's Twelve Steps, including Narcotics Anonymous, Al-Anon, Overeaters Anonymous, Debtors Anonymous and Workaholics Anonymous. And according to AAC, the core of the sponsor relationship is the same in every 12-step program. In Overeaters Anonymous, for example, sponsorship is one of the nine tools of recovery.

What researchers have found

The Recovery Research Institute publishes plain-language summaries of addiction studies. Its summary of the research on sponsors puts it plainly: with few exceptions, studies agree that having a 12-step sponsor is linked to better recovery from alcohol and other drugs.

One of those studies, published in 2016, followed 302 young adults aged 18 to 24 for a year after they left residential treatment. About two-thirds had a sponsor at some point during that year. The more contact people had with their sponsor, the more involved they were in 12-step groups. People who had a closer bond with their sponsor, meaning they felt liked and were on the same page about what they needed to do, had more days without drinking or using. The researchers point out that this shows a link and doesn't prove the sponsor was the cause.

What they agree on

All four sources describe a sponsor as a fellow member rather than a professional, and the study suggests the relationship itself makes a difference. AA and AAC are also honest that a sponsor is one person with a life of their own. That's why AAC asks sponsees to keep calls to a minimum, and why AA's pamphlet covers what to do when you can't reach your sponsor.

For those times, AA's advice is to lean on the whole program, because in AA's view it's the program, not any one person, that keeps you sober. Call or text other members, go to a meeting, phone your local AA office, or read or listen to AA literature on aa.org. The pamphlet also asks sponsors to be open to texting instead of relying only on phone calls, for members who find calls difficult.

How to find a sponsor

AA describes finding a sponsor as informal. Often you just ask a more experienced member you feel comfortable with. There's an old AA saying, "stick with the winners," which here means looking for someone who seems to be living the program and enjoying their sobriety.

AAC adds that at the end of many meetings, the leader asks anyone willing to sponsor to raise a hand. If your meeting doesn't do that, let the group know you're looking, or ask someone before or after a meeting. If they say no, try not to take it personally. They may just not have the time. AAC's advice is to keep asking.
